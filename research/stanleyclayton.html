Stanley Clayton's Escape From Jonestown

A Survivor's Story

As a young man, Stanley Clayton was looking for a way out of addiction. What he found was a community that promised purpose, belonging, and a new beginning. Stanley Clayton was in his twenties when he found himself in the middle of the events of 18 November 1978 in Jonestown Guyana. His testimony provides crucial evidence about the coercive nature of the deaths of 909 Peoples Temple members in Jonestown. The following is a comprehensive account of the testimony provided by Jonestown survivor Stanley Clayton to the Federal Bureau of Investigation in January 1979. All quotes from Stanley Clayton are taken from the PBS American Experience documentary, Jonestown: The Life and Death of Peoples Temple and Deleted Scenes Bonus Interviews

(Reich Family) 09/04/26 - Stanley Clayton's journey into the Peoples Temple began in 1971 when his mother, who occasionally attended temple meetings in the San Francisco area, suggested he accompany her to a gathering. His mother believed the church could help him improve his life, and though she eventually lost interest and stopped attending, Clayton remained. Stanley Clayton joined the Peoples Temple looking for a way out. He had been using drugs and drinking heavily, and when he saw young people his age who were clean and sober, it struck him. "I was one of those kind of guys that ... I used drugs. I was an alcoholic. I drunk alcohol and stuff like that. And ... and all these people that were like my age, they were clean." For him, that was enough. "And for me, that was like, 'Wow, man.' I liked that." He went home and told his mother, "You know what, this is the right church for me." The next week, he became a member of Peoples Temple. In the early days, there was a sense of purpose. Clayton remembered getting dressed up for meetings. "We all got suited down, neck-tied and everything. You know, and we were sharp." He found community, a place to belong.

He moved into the temple's communal settlement in Redwood Valley, California, where the facility functioned as both a church and a farming community. Clayton understood that by joining the settlement, he was committing to become a member of the church's community, and in exchange, the temple provided all necessary food and lodging. His only requirements were to attend religious meetings and reside at the settlement. Clayton remained at the Redwood Valley settlement for approximately nine months before returning to his hometown, having obtained his high school diploma during his time there. He wanted to "show off" his achievement, and his departure from the temple seemed amicable enough, as he severed his affiliations with the Peoples Temple from the summer of 1971 until March 1975.

In March 1975, Clayton's mother again expressed concern about his general behavior and once more suggested he affiliate himself with the temple. Clayton rejoined the Peoples Temple and moved back to the settlement in Redwood Valley, where he performed various duties in the general area surrounding the settlement, with the work being sponsored by temple funds. Sometime in the spring or summer of 1975, Clayton was relocated from the Redwood Valley settlement to the Peoples Temple in Los Angeles, where he lived in an apartment within the temple building. While in Los Angeles, he continued working on various projects and was specifically assigned certain duties. However, in August 1975, temple leader Reverend Jim Jones decided that Clayton should be returned to the San Francisco facilities because of what was described as his "bad attitude." This decision was made after a conference between Jones and Clayton's supervisor at the Los Angeles temple, and Jones would make trips from San Francisco to Los Angeles approximately every other week to discuss matters with temple leadership there.

Upon his return to San Francisco, Clayton lived at the temple property located on Divisadero Street, where he performed no chores other than keeping his room clean. In the autumn of 1976, Clayton re-entered school, but he noted that this decision, like all significant decisions for temple members, was made by Jim Jones.

But life in the Temple was not always as it appeared. Discipline was public and brutal. "There wasn't a week that went by that I wasn't called up on the floor because of my behavior, because of my attitude. 'Stanley Clayton, up, front, center.'" The beatings were routine. "You might fight five people in one night. Well, you know, you're very tired! I've seen situations where they actually knocked the person out and actually took water and threw water back on him, woke him up, and whooped him some more."

In March 1976, Jones departed for Guyana with two other temple members, and Jones told Clayton that he could attend school once he got to Guyana. Clayton made the trip to Guyana by commercial air carrier in August 1977 after being given a ticket at the San Francisco temple. He flew by airplane, though he could not recall the exact airlines, to New York Airport and from there to Guyana and Georgetown. In New York, he met with three Johnson sisters from the Los Angeles temple, who were also on their way to Guyana. Among them was Janice Johnson, who was Clayton's partner. When traveling, Clayton took only his personal belongings, which consisted of a double duffel bag of clothes and an artist's drawing that someone had asked him to give to somebody in Jonestown, though he could not recall who the intended recipient was. The painting was framed and measured eighteen by twenty-four inches, and he eventually gave it to somebody in the kitchen at Jonestown, who furnished it to the intended recipient. Clayton stated that he took only thirty-six dollars of his own money to Guyana and had no knowledge regarding any other monies being taken to Jonestown from the San Francisco temple.

When Clayton and the Johnson sisters arrived in Georgetown, there were already approximately eighty temple members there. Clayton remained behind at the temple facilities in Georgetown for one month while the other members proceeded to the Jonestown settlement. He was required to stay behind because he had to accept a load of wood that was arriving for the settlement. During his first week in Georgetown, he stayed at the temple house in Lamaha Gardens, and for the remaining three weeks, he remained with the boat that contained the wood, which was specifically used to transport supplies to the settlement. Upon his arrival in Guyana, Clayton also stayed for about one week at the temple residence in Georgetown and then stayed on a boat called the Lafayette, which was not the temple boat at that time but was located about one-quarter mile away from the temple boat in the same harbor or boat mooring area. He recalled that Tim Sweeney was in charge of this boat at that time and that another individual named Cleveland Newell also stayed on the Lafayette. To his knowledge, Newell survived the massacre and is still alive, though Clayton did not know where he was at the time of the interview.

After four weeks in Georgetown, Clayton finally proceeded to Jonestown on the wood-hauling boat with other temple members, including Shirley Smith, Becky Flowers, Patsy Johnson, and Shirley Baisey with her four children. Upon their arrival, the group was greeted by Jones and members of a welcoming committee, with approximately twenty-five temple members travelling on the boat with him. At the time of his arrival, Clayton estimated that the settlement contained about four hundred members. Clayton was given a speech of welcome by Jones upon his arrival, during which Jones explained the purpose of the Jonestown settlement and what the new arrivals could expect living there. However, Clayton later discovered that the purposes and expectations as set forth by Jones did not match the reality of life in the settlement. He was assigned to work in the kitchen under the direction of a supervisor, and his sole responsibility was to work in that capacity seven days a week. He and others who were considered not to have conformed to the rules of the settlement were guarded by a guard with a shotgun who threatened to shoot the group members on various occasions. Clayton also admitted that he received beatings on occasion for his poor attitude and overall behaviour. Clayton stated that he was not a member of the settlement's basketball team but observed that approximately thirty guns were in the possession of various members of the temple security force during his time in Jonestown.

Clayton described the phenomenon of "white nights," which began sometime in September 1977 and continued until December 1977. These were meetings called by Jones to instill discipline in settlement members, during which everyone was put on alert and ordered to report to the main pavilion where Jones addressed them regarding the necessity for them to commit suicide. The meetings seemed to die out for a time and then started up again. During one White Night meeting on April 12, 1978, Jones publicly berated Clayton and Janice Johnson, attacking their relationship in front of the entire community. Jones was furious that Janice had chosen to be with Stanley instead of another member, Dr. Larry Schacht, and used the opportunity to lecture the crowd about personal attachments. Jones called Stanley the "class enemy" for having dealt drugs in San Francisco. The crowd grew so angry that Jones had to tell them not to tear Clayton's clothes because they would need to be replaced.

Clayton advised that he was not aware of any plans to kill any outsiders while in Jonestown. He was aware, however, that Jones was notified of the arrivals and impending arrivals of all persons from the outside, and when these notifications came, efforts were made to fix the place up to prepare for their visits. Messages were relayed through Georgetown to Jonestown regarding these arrivals. With respect to Congressman Leo Ryan, Clayton advised that there was talk that some relatives and Ryan were coming to Jonestown in about a week, and he recalled that Jim Jones stated that he did not want them coming, nor did he want any reporters either. Clayton described the whole situation as rather mixed up and confused as to what was going to happen upon their arrival. He stated that he was not aware of any so-called "contingency plans" and did not recall receiving instructions regarding pre-planned assassinations, but he did recall that members were expected to fight should the church or its members be attacked. In Jonestown, the rule was that if someone from the outside came into the community, everyone was to report to the main pavilion and drink poison Kool-Aid, which is what happened when Congressman Ryan and his party had left. Clayton advised that Jones never said exactly what would happen to the survivors, but left this matter undecided. Clayton also noted that he overheard in conversations with other temple members and from Jones himself prior to the mass deaths at Jonestown that if Jones died, others would take care of the temple members who were left.

When Congressman Leo Ryan arrived in Jonestown on November 17, 1978, Clayton was among those who greeted him. "When Ryan came, he came on a Friday night and we put on a reception for him." The mood was hopeful. "Everything up to that point was, was ... was good. Everybody was thrilled that Ryan was thrilled. He just kind of praised us."

The following morning, Clayton sensed something was wrong. "I was like the first to rise up the following morning. It was a bright sunny day, but it was a dark day. It just didn't feel right."

On the afternoon of November 18, 1978, Clayton was occupied with his duties in the settlement kitchen when he heard that there had been a shooting at the Port Kaituma airstrip after the return of some temple members from the airstrip. Later that evening, around 4:30 PM or later, Clayton recalled that Dr. Larry Schacht and nurse Joyce Touchette came to the kitchen and took a large cooking tub to the pavilion. He recalled seeing in one or both of their possessions a box of "Flavor-Aide," which is similar to Kool-Aid but a different brand, and they also had two plastic brown-colored labeled jars, though he could not read what was on the labels. At that point, Clayton recalled that the kitchen workers were ordered by one of the security men, Lou Jones, an Asian or possibly Chinese man, to go to the pavilion. Jones had a gun in his waist tucked under his belt, but he did not take it out or point it at any of them. The seven people in the kitchen at that time consisted of Mary Rogers, Hazel Newell, Maurice Jackson, David Sullivan, Gloria Rosa, Kim Fye, and Betty Jean Gill.

After Ryan departed, the community was ordered to the pavilion. Jones told the crowd they had to die. "He said, 'Well, we got to go. We got to get out of here. We got to ... we got to go to sleep. Get the solution together.'" Jones came down from the podium. "He said, 'Hey, we got to do this. We got to ... we got to go, that if we don't go this way, we going to go like this.' They were coming, taking like newborn babies out of their mothers' arms."

When they arrived at the pavilion, Clayton observed that people were being forced to drink the poison and were also willingly drinking the poison drink. He observed several people being forced to drink the poison, noting that most of those forced to drink were the "seniors" or older people at Jonestown. Specifically, he saw two security men, Jim McElvane and Bruce Turner, force two seniors, Freddie Lewis and Nancy Jones, to drink the poison liquid. He observed Jim Jones use force on many of the seniors and chastised them for not coming forward to die with the rest. Clayton saw many older people being forced to drink the poison or being injected with poison by the doctor and the nurses. He did not know of anyone who was involved in the killings or the forcing of the older people who was alive at present.

In his testimony before the coroner's inquest, Clayton recalled Jones beseeching the reluctant followers: "He kept telling them, 'I love you. I love you. It is nothing but a deep sleep. It won't hurt you. It's just like closing your eyes and drifting into a deep sleep.'" He also testified that Jones's wife, Marceline, walked among the followers, hugging them and saying, "I'll see you in your next life."

At first, Clayton said, many in the commune seemed to think they were participating in one of Jones's "white night" drills, not actually taking poison. But after the babies were gone, people began to realise what was really happening, and many became reluctant to continue. It was at this point that Jones stepped into the crowd with security guards and began pulling people from their seats, guiding them toward the poison vat.

Clayton detailed how nurses first administered poison to babies, with some mothers bringing their own children forward. In his testimony to a coroner's jury, Clayton described Jones coming down from the stage with security guards to pull reluctant followers from their seats when many resisted. He witnessed one woman injected in the arm after struggling against the poison. One elderly woman, Christine Miller, asked why they couldn't flee instead to Russia.

In the chaos, Clayton found himself holding a young boy named Thurman. "There was a young kid, his name was Thurman and when he came inside, he bumped into me. At that same time, he's falling to the ground and he's going into convulsion." Clayton grabbed him and carried him out of the pavilion. "I grabbed the kid from the shoulders up, and in that process of taking him out of the pavilion, this kid died in my arms. I mean, I just felt the life go out of him. To me, at that point, I knew that this shit was real."

In Jonestown: The Life and Death of Peoples Temple, Deleted Scenes, Bonus Interviews , Stanley expressed, "My wife went up to that Kool-Aid, to that death barrel and she just, didn't hesitate, just took it and drunk it and told me to hold her, to take her, and I did. And she died in my arms. And once I laid her down and she told me how she wanted to lay with her grandmother, I um, at that point, knew that I didn't have no reason to be here no more. And I kind of grabbed a hold of myself and just was looking for a way out. I was sitting on a seat and there was a guy that was next to me and they came up and pulled him out. I thought they were coming to get me, but they actually came and got him and made him drink this stuff. The conclusion that I came to was that I was either going to have to knock this security guard out or take this poison. And I'm not going to take this poison. And so I was in the process of getting ready to knock this security guard out, which was a lightweight. He wasn't nobody. And I mean, you know, I was very athletic, fast. You know, give me about 10 feet, you can't catch me. You know, if you do, I'm going to tear you off. They was asking us to embrace each other, you know, to comfort each other and stuff like that. And so I'm on the road where I'm getting ready to knock this man out and be gone. But in doing so, as I'm checking behind me to see where these people are at with this barrel, with this poison coming, I happen to just see there is an opening, which is an entrance into the school. Instead of me coming around to hit him, I come around and embrace him with a smile, because I just found my key on how to get out of here. And I, you know, and I went through the process. Yes, I love you too, Ray, and woo, woo, woo. And hey, Ray, I'm fixin' to go through the school here and talk to the people who's up at the front where they're making the solution at. And once he gave me the okay to do so, I said, adios. I just actually took my time and walked through the entrance to the school, out to the field. Once I got out in the field, I just hauled ass to the jungle. I sat there for about an hour because they were still in the process of taking that stuff. When I came out, I wound up in the area where I lived at. So I went up in the area and got some clothes and stuff like that. I had to go back through the community to get to the road to walk up out of there. I had to go over the dead people." Clayton has never called what happened that day suicide. "I ain't never used the term 'suicide,' and I'm not gonna never use the term 'suicide.' That man killed ... was killing us."

After the deaths seemed to end and silence fell on Jonestown, Clayton, hiding in the bush, heard what he described as a chorus of cheers from within the commune. "What I heard, I would say was three cheers. It sounded like a lot of people. It was just a lot of voices." When he later tried to sneak back to retrieve his passport, he heard five gunshots and dropped back into the jungle. Later, as he pulled the passport from an office file, he heard a sixth shot.

In his 1979 interview with the FBI, Clayton explained that his being alive and escaping unfolded as follows: while at the pavilion, he was able to get past a security guard at the entrance to the school pavilion where they were making the poison. A short time after the poisoning began, Dr. Schacht asked for a nurse to get him a stethoscope. Clayton and Odell Rhodes left the pavilion area to obtain a stethoscope from the hospital building. Rhodes walked with Clayton to the hospital, where he entered and then left Clayton exiting through the back door. Rhodes crawled under the hospital building and remained there until dark. Clayton ran into the jungle at about 7:00 PM. While hiding in the jungle, Clayton could hear shooting coming from the Jonestown compound. He also heard what he described as "three cheers" from within the commune approximately thirty to forty-five minutes after the suicide seemed to have ended. He came out of the settlement at about 1:00 AM and walked toward Port Kaituma. At some point before reaching Port Kaituma, he was questioned by Guyana authorities and remained in Georgetown at the Park Hotel, where his residence was paid for by the U.S. Embassy. Clayton stated that he never talked to anybody connected with the Jonestown massacre or persons who were reportedly involved in the killings of Representative Ryan and his party.

Clayton provided investigators with the names of numerous security men he was aware of, including Billy Oliver, Tom Kice, Bob Kice, Poncho Johnson, Ronny James, Ronnie Dennis, Danny Moton, Donnie Casanova who usually carried a crossbow, Earl Jones, Joe Wilson, and Bruce Oliver. On the night of the mass deaths, Clayton escaped from the settlement by slipping past a security guard armed with a crossbow. After escaping into the bush, Clayton kept wandering around until he came to the main road leading from Jonestown to the Port Kaituma airstrip. He went to a farmhouse where a Guyanese farmer directed him to other settlement survivors who were at the airstrip. He and the other survivors went from Port Kaituma back to Georgetown. Clayton was interrogated by Guyanese police, who released him after the interrogation.

On January 19, 1979, Clayton was interviewed immediately following his arrival by commercial air carrier from Georgetown, Guyana, to Miami International Airport. This interview was conducted in the presence of a U.S. Secret Service agent in the office space of the U.S. Customs Service. Clayton was advised that he was not under arrest, nor should he in any way consider the agent's contact with him as implying that he was in federal custody. He was further advised that any information he furnished was on a voluntary basis, and the interviewing agent advised Clayton of the purpose of the interview to elicit information regarding his associations with and knowledge of the Peoples Temple. Clayton stated he understood and willingly consented to be interviewed.

On January 24, 1979, Clayton was contacted by FBI special agents in California and advised of their official identities. He was informed that the purpose of the interview was to obtain any information regarding details relating to the assassination of Congressman Leo Ryan in Guyana. During this interview, when questioned by investigators, Clayton provided specific responses to their inquiries. He stated that he never had any weapons training or purchased any weapons and that, to his knowledge, only Johnny Jones, who was now dead, and Tim Jones, Jim Jones' son, were involved in the purchase of weapons. He had no information regarding any firearm training received by temple members, including specific dates, places, and trainers. He was aware of generalized threats against people who wanted to leave the temple but not of any specific threats. He was not aware of any money whatsoever being stockpiled by Jones either in Guyana or at temple locations. He denied any knowledge of contingency plans or discussions of plans reportedly espoused by Jones as a means of retaliation against any persons attempting to arrest or capture Jim Jones or who would interfere with church policies. He was not aware of any federal officials, international officials, or state officials who were targeted for murder. He had no information concerning bribery or influence peddling by members of the temple.

Clayton suggested that Mike Prokes and Johnny Jones might have been members of the Planning Commission, but he was not aware of anyone who was on the inner core of that commission. He furnished the names of individuals comprising the security force and advised that this force did have some people who were identical with people who were on the basketball team, including Steven and Tim Jones, Jimmy Jones, Cleveland Newell, Walter Williams, Mike Touchette, and Mike Cordell. Clayton had not heard the term "the Angels" and had no knowledge regarding these individuals. He had no information regarding the involvement of members of the temple in any criminal offenses, such as assault, homicide, or fraud. The only person he knew to have been a radio operator was Mike Carter, and he did not know anything regarding any radio codes.

Regarding the assassination of Congressman Ryan and his party, Clayton stated that he did not know anything regarding any of the questions posed and was not involved, nor did he know of any planned practice drills or who was involved in the assassination. He confirmed that there were practice suicide drills when people pretended to drink the poison, but he was uncertain if narcotics were used to facilitate this. He witnessed the forced drinking of poison and the use of injections of narcotics to murder members at Jonestown. He denied knowledge of the existence of an assassination squad and the identity of any members, and he did not know the identity of any individuals who were given any assassination plans to carry out against survivors or defectors.

At 3:45 PM on January 24, 1979, Clayton was served a subpoena from the U.S. District Court for the Northern District of California ordering him to appear before a grand jury. He acknowledged receipt of the subpoena and was furnished a copy along with the Grand Jury subpoena attachment, which advised him regarding his rights. He was advised that this Grand Jury inquiry was regarding possible violation of Title 18, US Code, Section 351, the Congressional Assassination statute. Clayton was also advised that should he remember any additional details that he felt important to furnish to the FBI, he could contact the special agents and was furnished with a telephone number. The FBI reports note that all questions intended to be asked were not posed to Clayton during the Miami interview as he had to board a commercial air carrier for his final intended destination of San Francisco. The investigation into the Jonestown massacre and the assassination of Congressman Leo Ryan would continue for years, with survivors like Stanley Clayton providing crucial eyewitness testimony to help authorities understand the full scope of the tragedy.

Clayton was interviewed by U.S. authorities upon his return to Miami in January 1979 and was fully cooperative. His account has been featured prominently in the documentary Jonestown: The Life and Death of Peoples Temple and in Julia Scheeres' book A Thousand Lives: The Untold Story of Hope, Deception, and Survival at Jonestown. Time magazine covered his story as well.
